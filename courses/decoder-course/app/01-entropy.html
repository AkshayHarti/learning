Prediction is compression

A model that can guess the next character cheaply has compressed the text, because prediction and compression are both paid for in bits.

2 · Where the bits come from

Each bar is one symbol's share of the string. The entropy is the average surprise per symbol: rare symbols cost more, common symbols cost less. A uniform alphabet of 64 symbols would make every character cost exactly 6 bits, which is why that is the honest baseline to beat.

3 · One guess

The model assigns a probability p to the character that actually comes next. The price of that guess is its surprisal, −log₂ p. Drag the confidence and watch the price move non-linearly.

4 · Read the numbers

H·N is the information floor: no encoding of this exact string can do better on average. Perplexity is 2^H, the size of a uniform alphabet that would be equally hard to guess from. A model with perplexity 8 is choosing between eight equally likely characters at every step.

A real LLM does not see characters. It sees tokens, and its bits-per-token are compared against a much larger branching factor. The accounting is identical; only the unit changes.

Why this matters

Training a language model is literally minimising cross-entropy: the average surprisal it assigns to the next token in real text. That single number explains why models get better at everything at once when they get better at prediction — better compression means a richer internal model of grammar, facts and style. It also explains why "just make it bigger" works for a while: more parameters can store more of the regularity that prediction rewards. And it explains why a model that is confidently wrong is expensive twice: it pays a large loss during training and it emits nonsense at inference.
